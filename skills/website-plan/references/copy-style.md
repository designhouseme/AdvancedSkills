# Copy style for a business website

Read this when writing the section copy (point 6 in SKILL.md). The rules apply in any language; write in the language of the business's customers.

## Tone and sentences

- The voice shows the job instead of rating it: it answers the customer's question in the first line, names the work, the place and the next step, and leaves judgements like "reliable" or "careful" to the facts and the reviews. Adjectives describing the tone ("calm", "competent") make models write text that rates itself ("dependable", "steady"), so describe what the copy does.
- The most important point at the start of the paragraph. One paragraph is one thought, usually 1–3 sentences.
- Mix short factual sentences with longer explanations and conditions. Let the thought set the length; don't target a uniform word count or make every section the same size. Split a sentence when the reader has to reread it.
- Everyday, concrete verbs: we measure, we fit, we repair, we call back.
- Active voice, speaking directly to the reader as "you". In languages with a formal and an informal "you" (e.g. Polish ty/Państwo, German du/Sie), choose one and stick to it.
- Explain an industry term if it may be unfamiliar. In B2B keep professional precision; don't explain specialists' own industry to them.
- Read the copy aloud and remove manifesto rhythm, strings of clipped slogans and sentences nobody would say to a customer on the phone.
- Don't use the em dash (U+2014) in site copy. Prefer a period, comma, colon or parentheses. If a sentence needs a dash, use a standard hyphen (-); this is the house style, not a rule of Polish typography. Numeric ranges may keep their en dash.

## The company's voice, not the researcher's

Write a confirmed service directly: "We do ___", not "The company states that it does ___". Research caveats stay in the brief. A real review can carry its author and source. Don't turn a marketing claim into an objective fact.

## Proof in the copy

- Replace "ingredients from trusted sources" with the actual suppliers or ingredients, if confirmed. Add the reason for choosing them or a relevant test only when it is documented. Apply the same rule to methods, recipes, techniques and the people doing the work.
- Project: `job → approach → visible result`, from confirmed data only.
- Choose reviews for specifics (behaviour, timing, communication, result). You can shorten a quote with an ellipsis if it doesn't change the meaning. Don't embellish.
- Years in business, numbers, prices, ratings, guarantees and qualifications go in only as facts with a Source ID.
- Without reviews, use other proof: the process, the person responsible, a photo of the work, a qualification, a transparent scope.
- Put each piece of proof where it answers a specific doubt. A named client shows a relationship, not a result you haven't documented. Repeat a key message where it helps orientation, using relevant new support instead of copying the same quote everywhere.
- A real first-person story can explain a method or a choice. Keep the owner's experience and stance as given; don't invent anecdotes, blame old providers or promise admiration from other people.

## The offer

Group services by the customer's situation, not by the company's internal structure. Each description answers: when you need it, what the company does, what you get. Stick to the scope matrix from the research: don't narrow the offer to one job, and don't widen it based on a directory category.

One precise professional detail builds more trust than a paragraph about "the highest quality".

Show the outcome the customer cares about in ordinary language, then how the confirmed scope supports it. Let the reader recognise their own situation. Status or other people's reactions belong only when relevant to that audience; they aren't a default motive. List what is actually included so the value can be assessed. For offer design and incentives, use [offer-design.md](offer-design.md).

## Contact

Say exactly what happens after a call or a form submission: who handles it, what they do, what the customer needs to prepare and any confirmed timing. Don't promise a response time without a source. Show a real contact person's name and photo when available and appropriate; don't invent a host to make the form feel personal.

Choose one primary action at each decision point. The button names it, and nearby copy explains the next step and benefit; add a deadline only when real. A quiet alternative can serve someone who needs another channel without competing for attention.

For a form, record whether volume or qualification matters more. Keep only fields necessary to respond or qualify the request, explain unusual ones, and reuse details already supplied. Prefer one short step unless branching or necessary qualification justifies more. Treat conversion effects as hypotheses, not guaranteed uplifts.

Plan the confirmation or thank-you page too. Confirm what was actually submitted, explain what happens next and offer a useful continuation supported by the brief (such as selecting a time or preparing measurements). Make optional steps clear. An additional purchase isn't a condition of receiving what was already requested unless that condition was disclosed before submission.

## FAQ

3–6 questions drawn from reviews, purchase risks and typical worries. The answer starts with "yes / no / it depends" or the conclusion, then the condition. The FAQ doesn't repeat the list of services and isn't a place for keywords. Don't publish notes like "the company doesn't publish a price list"; give a useful next step.

## Banned phrases

Read this in the edit pass, after the draft, not before writing: a list of phrases read before drafting pulls the draft towards them. The full catalogue with replacements is in the copy-without-slop skill (`references/patterns.md`). Remove them or prove them with specifics:

- "top quality", "professional service", "tailored approach",
- "comprehensive services/solutions", "a wide range", "industry leader",
- "passionate about", "we make your dreams come true", "we exceed expectations",
- "a dynamic, fast-growing company", "years of experience" without a date,
- "elevate", "seamless", "unlock", "look no further", "your one-stop shop",
- "explore our services", "learn more", "contact us" as the default CTA,
- labels instead of theses: "About us", "Services", "Why choose us", "Our values",
- rhetorical questions with an obvious answer, exclamation marks, fake urgency.

Don't open with the company's history if the customer first wants to know whether the company will solve their problem.
