# Material and voice

Read this before drafting anything longer than a few lines (point 2 in SKILL.md).

## The brief

Keep it in a working note (a file or your own reasoning), not in the reply; the user needs only the gaps and the questions:

```md
Reader and moment: who, coming from where, wanting to know what
Facts: what / where / for whom / how it works / what happens after contact / what they don't do
Numbers: each with where it comes from
Customers' words: verbatim phrases from reviews, emails, calls
Proof: named reviews, photos, qualifications, process steps, guarantees as stated
The one thing only they can say:
Gaps: what's missing and whom to ask
```

Customers' words are the best raw material for headlines. Sort the phrases you find into what they liked, what annoyed them about other options and what worried them before buying. A phrase that repeats across reviews is a headline candidate. Competitors' reviews count too: their customers' complaints show what this business can answer.

## When facts run short: the ladder

Go down a step only when the one above is empty:

1. a fact with a source (a number, a name, a date, a review);
2. a process or scope the owner confirmed ("we measure on site before quoting");
3. the concrete next step ("call, and we'll tell you the price before we set off");
4. leave it out, or write `[MISSING: what]` and ask.

Never a precise-looking detail without a source. "Same-week appointments" from the notes beats "appointments within 48 hours" from nowhere.

## Inferences

An inference is a sentence the material makes likely but doesn't state: "so you don't wait for anyone else to sign it off", "big enough to slice a whole loaf". It may be true, and the owner may not want to promise it. Leave it out of the text and list it under "to confirm" in the reply, with the sentence ready to paste.

## Voice as values

Describe the voice by what it does, with values the draft can be checked against:

- who speaks: "we", "I", a named person, the company in the third person;
- address: you; in Polish ty or Państwo (`references/polish.md`), the same everywhere including buttons and forms;
- sentence length: e.g. mostly 8–18 words, none over 25 (Polish 20);
- 3–5 words the customers use for the thing (from reviews, not from the industry);
- 3–5 words this business would never say;
- a stance: who it's not for, what it doesn't do, a method it chose. Only from the owner. A refusal ("we don't fit laminate, only solid wood") gives a text more character than any adjective.

## Directions, when there's no voice yet

For a prominent text without a brand voice to follow, write 2–3 candidates that differ in substance, not in wording: one built on the service and place, one on a result, one on the strongest proof. Score each for clarity, credibility and whether a competitor could say it, and keep the winner. Show the user the candidates you didn't pick in one line each.

## Memory

If the project keeps notes (a brief folder, a project file), add one line per text: the voice decision and the element that belonged only to it. The next text for the same client keeps the voice; the next text for a different client starts from something else.
