# What a good brand book contains

Read at the direction stop, before you plan the pages. Based on published brand guidelines (NASA 1976, IBM, Spotify, Slack, Uber, Mailchimp, Monzo, Greenhouse), print and packaging specifications, and articles on why brand books go unused; checked October 2026. Public guidelines disappear, so the useful parts are written down here instead of linked.

## 1. Who reads it

The users of an identity system aren't designers. They're the people who produce materials after the designer has left: a marketing coordinator, a sales manager, an agency hired later, working in a hurry and with no design background (The Branding Journal, June 2026). Two tests from the same source belong in every project:

- **Before handover:** someone who didn't work on the brand makes a real piece (a post, a leaflet) using only the book. Every hesitation is a gap.
- **After six months:** see what was used and where the brand drifted. That is the brief for the next version.

The book's reader filter (owner, designer, printer, marketing) exists for these people: send each of them the link to their view.

## 2. Names: what the client is asking for

| Name | Scope |
|---|---|
| Księga znaku (logo manual) | the logo only: versions, colours, clear space, minimum size, misuse |
| Visual identity system (SIW) | the logo plus colour, type and applications: stationery, slides, signage, packaging |
| Brand book | all of that plus strategy and voice: why the brand exists, whom it speaks to, how it sounds |

Polish public procurement splits a "basic" logo manual (construction, CMYK/RGB/Pantone, clear space, mono and negative versions, minimum size, misuse examples) from an "extended" one with applications. It's a good checklist for a small brand too. Say in the direction stop which scope you are building.

## 3. Five layers, one question per page

Strategy, voice, visual identity, applications, then technical matters and governance: the order of good books for decades (NASA starts with a letter from the administrator and ends with vehicles; Slack has "Defining our brand", "Design elements", "Governance"). The template's part 1 is the brand (strategy, voice, applications), part 2 the rules.

| Section | The reader's question |
|---|---|
| Strategy | Why does the brand exist, in one sentence? For whom, instead of what? What do we do when the book doesn't say? |
| Voice | What stays the same and what changes with the channel? Which words do we avoid? How do we write the name? |
| Applications | What does a post, a card, a mail look like? (Shown as mockups, not described.) |
| Logo | Which version where? How much space around it? How small can it go, on screen and in print? What not to do? |
| Colour | Which values for which medium, in what proportions, and which text works on which background? |
| Type | Which face for what, which sizes, what to use in Word or Canva? |
| Imagery | What does our photo look like: light, frame, people, props, styling, colour? May we use AI, and how is it marked? |
| Labels | What goes on the front and in what order? Where are the zones? What content is mandatory and who approves it? |
| Print | Which profile, PDF standard, bleed, finishes? What to send the printer? |
| Files | Where is the right file, and which ones can go to print as they are? |
| Decisions | What is still open, who decides and by when? What changed since the last version? |

A brand story and a technical manual have different readers; big brands add a short guide for partners (Uber's was 10 pages in 2018; IBM's partner logo guide is 27). For a small brand, one book in two clear parts is enough. An agency case study is a third genre: it tells the project to future clients of the agency and has no rules, so don't build the book's pages from one ("Client, Industry, Scope" and a trophy number belong in a portfolio).

## 4. Numbers you can check

| Topic | Rule |
|---|---|
| Clear space | a part of the mark, called x, drawn on a diagram (Spotify: half the icon height; IBM: the height of the "I"; Uber: the cap height of the "U"); choose an element present in every version |
| Minimum size | in pairs: px for screen, mm for print, separately for the full logo and the symbol (Spotify: 70 px or 20 mm; the icon 21 px or 6 mm); a small-use version when the mark breaks down (Slack: 50–90 px wide) |
| Foil and embossing | the minimum follows from the thinnest line the finish can hold; confirm with the converter |
| Colour | HEX and RGB for screens, CMYK with its profile, Pantone C and U; rich black for large areas, 100% K for small text |
| Palette | 1–3 core colours, one accent, neutrals, written proportions; secondary colours only for illustration and charts |
| Contrast | WCAG 2.2: 4.5:1 for text, 3:1 for large text (18 pt, or 14 pt bold) and graphics, 7:1 for AAA; logotypes are exempt, text next to them isn't |
| Type | one or two families that differ in structure, plus a substitute for Word, Google Docs and Canva |
| Text size | body 10–12 pt in print, 15–25 px on screen; lines of 45–90 characters |
| Mandatory text on food labels | x-height at least 1.2 mm, 0.9 mm when the largest surface is under 80 cm² (Regulation (EU) 1169/2011, art. 13) |
| Bleed | 3 mm for offset by default; digital and label printers often ask 1.5–2 mm; confirm with the printer |
| Resolution | 300 ppi at final size; 600+ for labels; line art 1200 ppi |
| The PDF of the book | selectable text, a clickable contents, version and date on the cover, small enough to email (aim under 20 MB) |

## 5. Patterns worth copying

- **NASA Graphics Standards Manual (1976):** the logo colour depends on the background in four classes (white, very light, medium, dark), and nine incorrect uses are written as full sentences with a drawing each ("The logotype should never be shown on a vertical axis"). A whole chapter of reproduction art: files, not pictures of files. An effective date on the first page.
- **Spotify:** minimums for both media; clear space as half the icon; the green logo only on black or white; six short don'ts.
- **IBM partner guide:** the positive and negative logos are drawn differently, compensating optically on dark backgrounds; a partner logo sits at least three clear spaces away.
- **Slack:** every colour in four notations; a matrix of allowed and forbidden text and background pairs; a darker blue variant just for links on white; a substitute font for office suites; extra room for longer languages.
- **Uber quick guide:** type sized from the logo (headline at least 1.5 times the logo, subhead half the headline; logo height equals the margin); one functional colour used only for safety moments.
- **Mailchimp:** voice stays, tone changes with the reader's state; traits written as "X, not Y" ("Plainspoken, not hyperbolic").
- **Monzo:** three voice principles with an intensity per channel; no wit at all in operational messages about money.
- **Greenhouse:** photography as rules for light, composition, casting, props, wardrobe and grading ("Natural light should be prioritized over artificial lighting"; "People should tell the story in our photos, not objects").

## 6. Why brand books end up unused

Too abstract (a founder's essay without rules), too long and rigid, hard to find, out of date, no real examples, no files, no don'ts, unreadable or unsearchable PDFs. Answers that are built into this skill: one question per page, rules as headlines, mockups from the tokens, files linked from the book, version and date on the cover, open decisions with people and dates, a reader filter instead of one long read. The often-quoted "95% of companies have guidelines, 25% enforce them" could not be traced to a primary source: don't use it.
